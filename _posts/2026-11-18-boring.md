</figure><p>They say that if you&#8217;re the smartest person in the room, you&#8217;re in the wrong room. But what they don&#8217;t tell you is it feels kinda bad to be in the top decile dumbest in the room. Never is this more clear than when I&#8217;m in Berkeley.</p><p>To be clear, I don&#8217;t think I&#8217;m all <em>that</em> boring. I&#8217;m intelligent, reasonably well-educated, I have Interests, I can hold conversations on a wide variety of topics. But Berkeley definitely makes you feel dumb. Different cities reward different things &#8211; NYC rewards social status, London rewards financial success, Berlin rewards coolness and esoteric interestingness. <a href="https://paulgraham.com/cities.html">Berkeley rewards intelligence</a>.</p><p>People self-select into a milieu that has similar interests to them. This might be through their work &#8211; I work at an effective altruist organisation, so there&#8217;s a base level of familiarity with certain domains of philosophy, as well as a common worldview. This could be through interest groups, or a university friendship circle, or political beliefs.</p><p>But when you find yourself in a milieu with very different interests to you, you&#8217;re exposed to people with a deep knowledge of areas you know basically nothing about. I can hold my own in a conversation about the merits of different GLP-1 receptor agonists, or the putative mechanism of SSRIs. I can show an interest in, but perhaps not contribute enthusiastically to, a conversation about AI safety. But talk to me about how republics are more successful than democracies and I have nothing to add &#8211; I can just stare at you blankly and be interested.</p><p>That&#8217;s not nothing, of course! &#8220;Taking an interest&#8221; (another useful phrase) is important. Lots of people will just think you&#8217;re a nerd for your interest in the Roman voting system and not entertain it. But I definitely can&#8217;t <em>hold my own</em> in such a conversation. I&#8217;m not going to be able to add much. I&#8217;m going to feel like a dumb boring bimbo.</p><p>People&#8217;s interests are spiky. Usually you have deep interests in a particular area, and shallow ones in others. Often this spikiness is predictable: a person interested in legal history probably doesn&#8217;t have a deep knowledge of molecular biology (though you never know, especially here).</p><p>This spikiness can exaggerate how boring you feel in a given crowd. I&#8217;m a software engineer, but software doesn&#8217;t come up much in conversation beyond saying that it&#8217;s a craft which is on the verge of being wholly automated. In this milieu, no-one wants to talk about union types or their thoughts on whether microservices were a mistake. Mostly because these days you just shit out some words that gesture at what you want and <a href="https://henryaj.substack.com/p/ai-comes-for-the-nerds">Claude builds it</a>. Even DHH, the creator of one of the most popular web frameworks in history, now says <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vDjW_dRyKXY">he doesn&#8217;t care about the code</a>.</p><p>In particular, if the things you know a lot about don&#8217;t come up in conversation, you can end up seeming dull. I find myself listening in to conversations about timeless decision theory, picking up snippets, keen to learn more about what they&#8217;re saying but too shy to actually contribute.</p><p>Native speakers of a particular language often seek the comfort of their mother tongue even when they speak other languages perfectly. Sometimes it&#8217;s nice to be around people that know roughly the same stuff as you, so you can trade friendly phatics instead of debating the merits of an economic philosophy you learned existed only five minutes ago.</p><p>But at the other side of seeming or feeling boring is the magic of discovering something new. If you can cross the <a href="https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/the-moat-of-low-status-68a">moat of low status</a> &#8211; of seeming like the dumbest guy in the room &#8211; you can experience again the pleasure of learning new things, of stretching your brain into a new conformation. There&#8217;s magic in talking to someone with very different interests from your own: the sparkle of learning something new, especially in a domain which you didn&#8217;t know anything about, maybe didn&#8217;t know existed. If you&#8217;re smart and interested and are willing to ask stupid questions and derive a few things from first principles, you can get a lot of joy from talking to people about new things. It feels like learning how to think.</p>
